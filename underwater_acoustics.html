Why Study Underwater Acoustics?

Acoustics are essential to studying biological, geological, and anthropogenic activity in the ocean. Particularly for biologcal actitivty: species presence, population estimates, species dynamics are all heavily dependant on stuydying acoustic signatures. Alternative methods include visual observations, where species dynamics are estimated based on human-observed surfacing behavior, or tagging, where sensors are fixed to animals and record kinematic and oceanographic data. These methods limited as animal behavior extends vastly beyond what can be seen during surfaces, surface events are sparse, and ship time is expensive. Tagging can potentially yield a lot of data, but requires animal surfacings, precise tag application, and is limited by battery life. Acoustics are the most fruitful method, underwater microphones, or hydrophones, can be deployed for months to years at a time and record nearly all sounds, including some that have propagated from across the globe.

Graphic displaying sources of underwater noise.Source

The Scripps Whale Acoustics Laboratory focuses primarily on marine mammals, but is also heavily invested in noise created by ships of various types. They, and other labs in the Marine Bioacoustic Research Collaboative have performed studies all over the world: locally in the Southern California Bight, further in the Pacific like Hawaii, the Atlantic ocean, Antarctica, and the Canadian Arctic. The lab developed their own hydrophones in house, they typically record continuously at 200kHz to resolve as many relevant frequencies as posssible.

My first project focused on low-frequency acoustics for a project where data was collected in the Canadian Arctic. The goal was to correctly identify and isolate certain baleen whale signals that are generally less than 100Hz. The ocean is very loud at these low frequencies, so the signals are caught up in a plethora of shipping, flow, and geologic noise.

Low-Frequency Signals in the Arctic

First, I will outline some examples of signals that are found at low-frequencies in the Arctic. These examples come from summertime months, but during other seasons, particularly when ice sheets cover the ocean, the soundscape is drastically different. I will show spectrograms of acoustic timeseries data, which decompose timeseries data into it's frequency components over a specified chunk of time. Every acoustic signal has a specific pattern in frequency space.

90-30 and 50-30 Hz downsweeps, up to 2 seconds long, typically associated with sei whales.

Humpback whale songs, typically in the 100-1500 Hz range.

Seismic surveys are short pulses under 50Hz, on nearly perfect intervals. Bands of noise between 50-100Hz are created by electronics on the hydrophone array and block real signals.

Flow noise created by strong currents interacting with the hydrophone.

Extreme noise from ships.

Upsweep and downsweep signals from marine mammals.
